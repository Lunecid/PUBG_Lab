\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

In a battle royale game, many teams land on one map and a safe zone shrinks in discrete phases until one team remains. Each phase changes which teams are still at risk, how much space is left, and how often teams meet. Predicting next-phase eliminations can support live overlays, post-match review, and player feedback, and it is a natural discrete-time survival problem whose intervals are the safe-zone phases.

Prior work on PUBG predicts survival or winners from different slices of the available information. Sagong et al.~\cite{sagong2025hunterCamper} predict, once per phase, whether a player survives into the next half-phase in solo matches; their only signal about other players is a count of players within 100 m. Sagong and Kim~\cite{sagong2025survivalSequences} feed per-phase strategy sequences to recurrent models. The KRAFTON \texttt{pubg-winprob} release~\cite{krafton_pubg_winprob} applies self-attention across squads at one time point, with counts of enemies and teammates within 150 m among its inputs. Interaction-graph models~\cite{wang2025higformer,ying2021graphormer,xenopoulos2021gnnSports,bisberg2022gcnWp} encode typed relations. These lines differ in data, label, split, and backbone, so it remains open whether differences reflect what the model sees or how it relates teams over time. In our literature search (October 2026), we did not find a study that separates the two under a shared label, split, and backbone, or one that represents the shrinking safe zone as an explicit object node.

We therefore audit and rebuild the labels, compare adapted implementations of these approaches with a phase-conditioned representation on identical rows, and decompose the structure into switches in one attention backbone: the phase axis, the team axis, relation biases, and a safe-zone object node. The configuration with all switches on is called PTR-Former (Phase--Team--Relation Transformer); it is the vehicle for the decomposition, not a claim of improvement over other models. The decomposition rests on four pre-specified research questions (the analysis plan was fixed in a timestamped commit before the experiments).

\begin{itemize}
\item[] \textbf{RQ1 (phase axis).} Does encoding each team's phase-by-phase history improve next-phase elimination prediction beyond its current-phase state?
\item[] \textbf{RQ2 (team axis).} When KRAFTON-style cross-team attention is applied to next-phase hazard, does it improve absolute elimination risk, within-match relative standing, or both?
\item[] \textbf{RQ3 (relation).} Do explicit battle-royale relations (proximity, combat escalation, zone co-pressure, and teammates) add information beyond learned cross-team attention?
\item[] \textbf{RQ4 (safe-zone object).} Does representing the shrinking safe zone as an explicit object node recover the information carried by hand-crafted zone and habitat features?
\end{itemize}
P4, the shuffled-relations contrast, is the only confirmatory test for RQ3; per-type and teammate contributions are exploratory.

Comparisons with external baselines and the analysis of data scale are additional exploratory analyses outside the research questions. All claims concern predictive information; none is a causal claim.

The contributions are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Audited labels and data.} Snapshot-based alive inference mislabels 11.4\% of team-phase training rows (positive precision 70.4\%). We give event-based labels with clock alignment. Bot-dominated (\texttt{airoyale}) matches and a map with non-player combatants are excluded, which leaves 13,948 matches in 12 map $\times$ perspective $\times$ team-size cohorts (Section~\ref{sec:problem}).
\item \emph{Representation matters most.} On identical rows, gradient boosting on our phase-conditioned representation scores +0.102 AUC above an implementation of Sagong et al. adapted to our task, and the attention model +0.103 (12 of 12 cohorts); against KRAFTON's official code adapted to our task the difference is +0.057 (9 cohorts) \todo{K2: state whether the KRAFTON gap lies in the input representation} (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
